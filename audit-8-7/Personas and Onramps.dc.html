Personas and onramps Four roles · four doors · one test

There is no algorithm. You seek and you find.

That's the best line in your notes and I'd build the product position on it. Everything below is held to your own test — does this reduce to an emotion fast enough that someone thinks “I want to be part of that”? Feature lists fail that test, so each persona ends in a want, not a benefit.

The position

No feed, no ranking, no reach

Everywhere else a creative posts, an algorithm decides who deserves attention. Here nobody is being scored — you look for a room, you find it, you walk in. That's a real product difference and a spiritual one, and it belongs on the homepage in those words.

The trust premise

Everyone here came for the reason you did

Your line, barely edited. It's the entire value of a bounded community, and it's why a creative will show unfinished work here and not on Instagram. Say it in the second person, early, before you explain a single tier.

The shorthand to resist

“An artistic LinkedIn for Christians”

People will say it and you shouldn't fight it in conversation — it's a fast handle. But never build the LinkedIn feeling: no connection counts, no endorsements, no ranked feed, no “open to work.” LinkedIn is where people perform availability. This is where people do the work.

01 — The four roles

Four, not three — Community Partners are their own persona

Your notes kept circling something the current three-door split can't hold: a coffee shop owner, a gallery, a library, a bar isn't writing a check — they're giving a room. Filing them under Patron loses the most distinctive thing you're building: a growing network of real places to put work in front of people.

01 / Creative

Anyone making something and putting it out into the world

Deliberately wide — no portfolio bar, no discipline list, no professional/hobbyist line. Writers, musicians, filmmakers, designers, photographers, and the person who has made one thing and is scared to show it.

02 / Host — rebuilt

A Kingdom-minded creative who leads a community

Your correction, and it's exactly the fix this persona needed: not an administrator who happens to serve artists, but a practitioner who gathers. They still make their own work. That's why other creatives follow them.

Primary function
Convening and discernment
Opportunities
Run tables without five separate tools · route patron money to the right people · get their organization credited on every work it touches
How does this help me…
…turn the people I already gather into something that outlasts my group chat.

“I want to be the one who makes that room.”
03 / Patron

Anyone who can point money at a person's work

Not only wealthy individuals and churches — a small business, a library, a community association, a bar owner, a family. The $10 covered seat and the $500 Fellowship are the same instinct at different scales, and today only the large end has a door.

Primary function
Funding and credit
Opportunities
Fund a named person rather than a program · a story page they can forward instead of writing a report · a visible place in their city's creative life
How does this help me…
…know exactly whose work my money made, by name.
Reduces to
“I want to be part of that story.”
04 / Community Partner — new

A local organization that backs the scene, not one person

Coffee shops, galleries, venues, libraries, bookstores, print shops, churches with a building. Patrons back a who; partners back a where. A place is the most common thing they offer — but not the only one, which is why the term is Partner and the offer is a separate field.

Primary function
Local infrastructure
Offers one or more of
Space — a wall, a night, a stage · Goods and services — printing, framing, gear, studio time · Audience — foot traffic, a mailing list · Money — sponsoring a table or a show
How does this help me…
…fill my room on a slow night with people who come back, and be known for it.
Reduces to
“I want to be the place this happens.”

03 — Two structural notes

Open to closed, and shades of explicitness

Table lifecycle

Open → closed is a progression, not a setting

Table Art Society runs public tables — artists facilitating, a topic each month, anyone can walk in, closer to an AA meeting than a class. Then some become committed cohorts. That's a funnel, and it's the healthiest onboarding in your whole system: nobody has to commit to nine months before sitting in once.

Build it as a first-class flow — an open table can spawn a closed one, carrying its people. Don't make hosts rebuild the group by hand.

The ecosystem has shades

Not everyone flies the flag, and that's fine

A host whose nonprofit isn't faith-based. A coffee shop owner who's a Christian but whose shop isn't a ministry. A gallery that just likes the work. If the platform forces every participant to present as explicitly Christian, you lose exactly the people who make this an economy rather than a club.

So: The Garden is explicit; participants choose their own volume. The shared conviction is in who's gathered, not in a badge everyone has to wear.

The tension to handle deliberately — you wrote “christian capitalism”

Money moving between believers reads one of two ways: mutual aid, or a closed market trading on shared faith. The difference isn't the mechanism, it's the direction — and your 50% rule is what keeps it honest, because it means everyone here is a patron of someone else, not just a seller. Lead with that and the commerce reads as generosity. Lead with listings and pricing and it reads as a niche marketplace with a fish on it.

05 — Community partners, teased out

Patrons back a who. Partners back a where.

That's the line that makes the two roles distinct, and it's why Partner is the better term than Place — a print shop offering ten free prints a month is a partner and has no room to give. Place is the most common offer, not the identity.

Offer type · space

A wall, a night, a stage

Coffee shops, galleries, bars, church halls, living rooms. The unit is one recurring slot — the third Thursday, the back wall for a month. Recurring beats one-off: it becomes a habit creatives can plan around.

Offer type · goods

Printing, framing, gear, studio time

The most underrated one. A print shop, a framer, a rehearsal room, a camera rental, a PA system. These are the costs that kill small projects, and they're cheap for the business to donate at the margin.

Offer type · audience

Foot traffic and a mailing list

A bookstore with 4,000 subscribers can do more for a launch than a $500 cheque. Treat attention as a fundable contribution and credit it the same way you credit money.

Offer type · money

Sponsoring a table or a show

Where partner and patron overlap — and that's fine. The difference is what they're funding: a patron funds Shua, a partner funds “songwriters' night at our place, every month.”

Why a business says yes

Not charity. A coffee shop owner who's a Christian gets three things they'd otherwise pay for: people in the room on a dead night, a reason to be in the local creative conversation, and a credit on work they can point at. Frame the ask as cheaper and better than advertising — because it is — and never as a donation.

The ladder — keep step one tiny

One-off (a single night) → Recurring (that night, monthly) → Anchor (the home of a host's tables, named on the page). Most partners should never be asked for more than step one. The ones who want steps two and three will ask you.

06 — Host economics

Hosts earn. That changes their whole pitch.

If a host can charge on top of the base, the Host page stops selling software and starts selling a living. That's a much stronger offer — and it answers the question I left open: hosts don't just pay you, they're your only persona with revenue upside.

The new pitch

“Get paid to gather the people you already gather.”

Compare that to “$50/mo for community tools.” One is a cost centre, the other is a side income for a practitioner who's already doing the work for free. This is the single biggest copy upgrade available on the Host page.

What I'd price

Take a cut, not a subscription

A host who charges for a table should pay you a percentage of what they collect and nothing else — drop the $50 for them. Charging rent and commission on your best acquisition channel is how you lose them to a group chat and Venmo.

Keep the 50% honest

Say plainly what a paid table does and doesn't feed

Half of membership dues funds projects. If host fees don't, say so in one sentence rather than letting people assume. The 50% claim is your most valuable asset and the easiest to accidentally muddy.

Two risks that come with paid tables

Charging for spiritual formation reads differently than charging for a photography cohort. Some hosts will price a discipleship table and it will feel wrong to people. The norm that protects you: every host must keep at least one free or open table. Money is never the only door into a community — which is also just the open→closed pattern, stated as a rule.

And the boring one: paying hosts means payouts, tax forms, refunds and disputes. That's real operational weight for a small team. Worth scoping before it's on the pricing page.

07 — The Buzz

Liveliness without a leaderboard

You want the place to feel busy without importing what makes feeds miserable. There's a clean way through, and it comes from one observation: algorithms exist to rank people. Events don't need ranking — they already have a time and a place. Sort by soonest and nearest and you get abundance with no judgement in it.

Rule 01

Surface events, not posts

A show Thursday. A table seating three more. A project 60% funded, closing Friday. Things with a clock attached generate urgency honestly — and nobody feels judged for not being featured, because they weren't competing.

Rule 02

Aggregate is atmosphere, individual is scoreboard

“41 projects funded this month · 9 shows this week · 6 new tables” makes the place feel alive. The same numbers per person — views, likes, followers — makes it a contest. Publish totals generously. Never put a count on a human.

Rule 03

If you feature, a person picks — and you say so

Editorial curation is the opposite of an algorithm, not a weaker version of it. Byline it: “chosen this week by Sarah, host at Table Art Society.” Rotate the chooser. That's a feature you can put on the marketing site.

Rule 04

Guarantee a visibility floor

Every new project and every new member gets a turn in a “just posted” rail — not because it earned attention, because it's new. Rotation, not ranking. This is what makes “nobody's first week is silence” a mechanism instead of a hope.

Rule 05

Proof from your own people, not from strangers

“Three people from your table are going” is warmer and more persuasive than “trending.” It's social proof drawn from your actual relationships, which is exactly what a bounded community has and a feed doesn't.

Rule 06

Count deeds, never applause

Seats covered, projects funded, shows played, collaborators joined. Every visible number should describe something someone did, not something someone received. That single filter kills most of the bad behaviour before you build it.

On the word “buzz” — settled

“This week's buzz” is the right answer, and better than either “This week” or “The Buzz” alone. It leads with the time — so a first-time visitor knows what they're looking at before they've learned any of your vocabulary — and keeps the word that carries the feeling. The possessive does quiet work too: the buzz belongs to the people making it, rather than being something the platform selects for you. And it survives the two-metaphor budget on a technicality worth taking: bees live in gardens.

Use it at two scales. Nav item: “This week.” Three words is long for a tab, and the nav is the one place brevity beats warmth. Page headline: “This week's buzz in San Diego.” That's where the phrase earns its keep — and naming the city is what makes it feel like a place rather than a feed.

Open questions

Three I can't answer from here

Does a partner need an account? The lightest version is a host listing “Bird Rock Coffee, third Thursdays” with no login at all — you get the map without building a second onboarding. The heavier version lets partners post their own offers. I'd start light and see who asks for more.

What's your cut of a host's table? Now that hosts earn, this is a real pricing decision and it sets whether hosting is a side income or a hobby. It also determines whether you need payouts infrastructure in v1 or can settle outside the platform at first.

Is “This week” city-scoped from day one? The buzz only feels real if it's local, and local only feels full if there's density. Two cities with real activity beats twelve with one event each — which may mean deliberately not launching everywhere.
