Brand framework One name · one system · two mockups

You don't have two styles. You have two companies.

Now that I can see both: this isn't a consistency problem you fix by picking a shared blue. A dark gradient tech network and a letterpress arts program don't average into a brand. You asked for one name and a framework — here's the call, the vocabulary underneath it, and two pages built in it.

The tell

Not one element crosses: not the mark, the palette, the typeface, the ground, the button, or the name. A creative who reads the flyer and then signs in lands somewhere that looks like it was built by a different organization — right at the moment they're deciding whether to trust you with a project they care about.

My recommendation

The Table.

It already runs your product

Tables of ten. Seats at the table. Help set the table. It's the noun your own copy reaches for when it needs to describe the actual unit of gathering — and the one metaphor that survived being written three different times by three different pages.

It seats the creative as a peer

This is the whole argument. Patronage has a condescension problem — a garden makes the artist something that gets tended. “A seat at the table” is already the phrase people use for access and standing in a creative economy. For an audience asking for support, that dignity is the product.

It describes both sides at once

Creatives take a seat; patrons set the table; leaders host it. One image, three roles, no second metaphor required. Garden gives you growth but no reciprocity. Exchange gives you reciprocity but no welcome.

Legible to a church, neutral to everyone else

The table is the most central image in Christian practice. A pastor reads it in a half-second. A secular arts funder reads only hospitality. That's the exact register you want: warm to outsiders, unmistakable to insiders.

The cost, honestly: “The Table” is a crowded name — churches, a consultancy, restaurants. You will not win it on search, and a bare trademark is unlikely. Two mitigations, both of which you'd want anyway: a fixed descriptor that travels with the name everywhere, and a domain that reads as a phrase rather than a label. Keep creatives.exchange — not as the name, but pointed at the site: it's a good campaign URL and a better one than thegarden.app.

Voice rule 01

Name the person and the city

Shua, a musician in San Diego, funded by a neighborhood church. That's your best sentence and it appears once. Every claim gets one — a name, a place, a funder.

Voice rule 02

Say the number on the page

$10. $50. $500. Half of every membership. You already do this and it's rare in this category — it filters the wrong people out before a call, and it reads as honesty.

Voice rule 03

Never “platform,” “marketplace,” “solution”

Say what happens instead: someone funds someone's work, and you can watch it get made. Keep the one-italic-phrase-per-headline device from the flyers — it's the best thing in the current writing.

04 — The visual system

Neither of the two, built from both

Keep the editorial structure of the flyers — serif headline, hairline rules, one emphasized phrase, real measure. Keep the contrast and confidence of the product. Drop the two things that date it hardest: the blue-purple-pink gradient and the 2×2 tech mark. The accent moves from cool to warm — oak, bread, a communion cup — which no competitor in Christian tech is using and which reads as made-by-hand rather than venture-funded.

If you say yes

The order I'd do it in

This week

Server-render the homepage with a real title, description, and OG image. Independent of the name decision, and it's the only thing currently costing you traffic.

Then

Lock the seven words. Do a find-and-replace across the product, the flyers, and the invite emails before you touch a pixel — vocabulary is cheaper to change than design.

Before the rename

Run a trademark screen on The Table in class 42 and 36. If it's unworkable, The Garden is the fallback and everything else here survives unchanged.

Then the pixels

Wordmark, type, one accent, no gradients — across marketing and the app in the same release. Don't ship the new brand to one surface only; that's how you got here.
