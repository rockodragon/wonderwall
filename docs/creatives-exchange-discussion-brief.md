# creatives.exchange — discussion brief

*For the conversation with prospective community partners and collaborators.*
*Draft v0.1 · 2026-08-13 · owner: Rick*

**How to use this:** §1–2 is the proposal. §3 is what we've already decided and would rather not relitigate (with the reasoning, so you can challenge it if it's wrong). §4 is genuinely open — your input changes the answer. §5 is where we expect disagreement, stated as honestly as we can, with a recommendation on each so there's something to argue with.

---

## 1 · The shift

Until now this was **The Garden** — one platform, one community, our own. The new shape:

> **creatives.exchange is the platform. Communities live on top of it and keep their own names, language, and goals. Creatives hold one account and move between communities.**

Founding communities in the conversation: **Abiding Practice**, **Table Art Society**, **The Rabbit Room**, and others. The Garden becomes one community among them rather than the name of the whole thing.

**This is not a change of thesis.** The original vision doc set the validation bar at *"4 aligned communities inside 12 months, at least one outside our personal network — otherwise we're a single-customer shop with extra abstraction layers."* Multi-tenancy was always the bet; what changes is that communities become **visible** instead of hidden behind one brand, and that happens now instead of later.

## 2 · What the model looks like

| Layer | What it is | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|
| **creatives.exchange** | Accounts, payments, entitlements, project + funding infrastructure, cross-community discovery | Us |
| **Community** (AP, TAS, Rabbit Room, The Garden…) | Its own name, look, language, goals, membership, tables, standards | The community |
| **Table** | One ongoing gathering with a roster — class, cohort, critique night, mentorship circle | The host who runs it |
| **Creative** | One account, one profile, one body of work — participates in as many communities as they want | The person |

The Skool comparison is deliberate: you join *communities*, and the platform is the plumbing. The difference is what the plumbing does — Skool moves content and payments; we move **funding to specific creative projects, in the open.**

## 3 · Settled (challenge if wrong, but we have reasons)

1. **One account, portable identity.** A creative's profile, portfolio, and funded work belong to *them*, and travel across communities. Communities never own the person.
2. **Money is never the only door.** Every community keeps at least one free, open table. Product-enforced, not a policy request.
3. **Funding is named and public.** Patrons back a specific person or project; every allocation is published on a ledger anyone can read without an account. This is the trust engine, and it's already built.
4. **A covered seat is a full seat.** When a church or sponsor pays for someone, that person's capabilities are identical to someone paying their own way. Sponsorship changes who pays, never what you can do.
5. **No algorithmic feed, no vanity metrics.** Sorting is by time and proximity. We don't publish follower counts or applicant counts anywhere.
6. **Communities are provisioned by us, not self-serve.** At this scale, onboarding a community is a relationship, not a signup form.

## 4 · Genuinely open — your input decides these

- **What a community actually needs on day one** to move its people off group chats and spreadsheets.
- **What you already have that shouldn't be replaced.** We'd rather integrate with your existing giving platform, mailing list, or curriculum tool than rebuild it.
- **How much shared discovery you want.** Should your members see what's happening in other communities by default, or only when they go looking?
- **What "showcase" means across communities** — one shared event, or each community's own with cross-invites.
- **Governance.** If communities shape the roadmap, how? Who decides what gets built?

## 5 · Where we expect disagreement

### 5.1 Shared vocabulary vs. each community's own language
We locked six words — Creative, Patron, Host, Table, Seat, Project — so the product would stay coherent. "Communities define their own language" cuts against that directly.

- **Option A — one vocabulary everywhere.** Coherent, cross-community legible; forces Rabbit Room to call its thing a "Table."
- **Option B — every word customizable per community.** Maximum belonging; cross-community browse becomes confusing, and every screen needs a lookup.
- **Option C — split the difference.** Communities rename the *gathering and format* words freely (Table → Chapter, Circle, Cohort). The *money* words stay fixed everywhere: Seat, Patron, Project, Fellowship.

**Recommendation: C.** Money words appear in receipts, tax conversations, and cross-community ledgers — ambiguity there is expensive and occasionally legally messy. Gathering words are pure culture, and culture is exactly what a community should control.

### 5.2 Does 50% of dues fund creatives outside your community?
Today's published split is **40% community · 50% creative project pool · 10% platform.** The pool is the most radical part, and an established org may balk at funding strangers.

- **Per-community pool** — your members' dues fund your creatives. Easiest yes.
- **Platform-wide pool** — strongest solidarity story, hardest sell.
- **Opt-in** — communities choose; some run 90/10 with no pool at all.

**Recommendation: per-community by default, cross-community opt-in.** Nobody funds strangers before they fund their own. The promise stays true, just scoped: *half your dues fund another creative in your community.*

### 5.3 If I'm in three communities, do I pay three times?
Skool charges per community. That's simple and scales revenue — and it punishes exactly the mobility we're claiming as the differentiator.

- **Per-community dues** — clean, familiar, revenue scales with belonging.
- **One seat, platform-wide standing** — mobility is real; communities still charge for cohorts and premium tables.
- **Hybrid** — free to belong anywhere, pay where you want member privileges.

**Recommendation: one seat.** But be honest that this caps revenue per person and forces us to solve *which community earns the 40%* when someone participates in several. **This is the hardest unsolved question in the model and the most likely thing to change after this conversation.**

### 5.4 Is the platform explicitly Christian?
Current copy says "Kingdom-minded creatives." If communities define their own goals and we want to be inclusive, we need to say plainly what the platform itself is.

- **Explicitly Kingdom-oriented**, all communities share it.
- **Neutral infrastructure**, communities carry their own values.
- **Kingdom-founded and open** — built by and for this community, welcoming to others.

**No recommendation. This is a values decision, not a product one, and it should be made by the people whose names are on it.** It does have product consequences: it determines whether the platform's own pages carry faith language, or only the communities' pages do.

### 5.5 Offers — or Offers and Asks?
Today one word covers what people make available: a back room, a print shop's spare capacity, a mailing list, coaching hours. But half of what a community actually trades is the mirror image — *I need a space for a shoot Saturday, I need a drummer, I need someone with a long lens.*

- **Keep Offers only.** Asking happens through Projects, which already carry a budget and a scope. Fewer objects — but "I need a room for one afternoon" isn't really a project.
- **Add Asks as a peer object.** Honest to how communities actually work; one more thing to build and moderate.
- **Rename the pair "Offers & Asks."** One section, two directions, one mental model.

**Recommendation: none yet — worth arguing.** The instinct that "Offers" covers only half the exchange is right, and the platform is literally named for exchange. The cost to weigh: an Ask is low-commitment, expiring, and often urgent, which is a different shape from an Offer (standing, recurring). They may belong on the same page without belonging in the same list.

### 5.6 What community are you in when you haven't picked one?
Three linked questions, and they turn out to be one question.

**Is there a default community?** Options: no default (the platform is the commons — you browse everything and join open tables anywhere, and joining a community is an explicit act); or a house community everyone lands in. **Recommendation: no default.** Making The Garden the landing spot gives one community unearned primacy over its peers and confuses a newcomer who never chose it.

**Then who gets the 40% host share when someone has no community?** Options: the platform keeps it (honest, but reads as self-dealing); it goes to the pool; or **the member names their home community and the share follows that choice, defaulting to the pool when they haven't picked one.**

**Recommendation: the member picks, and can change it.** This is the answer that makes the whole model cohere:
- No participation-surveillance metric is needed — the member simply says who their dues support, which is exactly the transparency the product already promises.
- It turns the 40% into a **recurring earned share instead of a finder's fee.** A community keeps that share by being worth being in, not by having recruited someone once.
- Unaffiliated members become the strongest version of the pitch: *if you haven't picked a community yet, 90% of your dues fund other creatives' work.*

**Can a community be free?** Yes — and this distinction needs to be said out loud, because it's the thing most likely to be misunderstood:

> **A seat is platform membership, not community membership.** A community can be entirely free to join. The $10/mo seat is what unlocks starting projects, applying to paid work, and joining members-tables — anywhere on the platform.

So a free community's people can stay free-account users indefinitely, and "money is never the only door" survives intact. What a community actually sells is its **cohorts and classes** (their price, they keep ~90%) plus the **dues share from members who name them home**.

**The honest catch:** a free community whose members never buy seats earns nothing, and so does the platform. That's acceptable — free communities are the top of the funnel — but somebody in the room will spot it, so we should say it before they do.

### 5.7 Who pays us, and why anyone pays at all

**Who pays a subscription: only members. Nobody else.**

| | Pays | Gets |
|---|---|---|
| Free account | $0 | Show up: browse everything, join open tables, attend events, have a portfolio |
| Member | $10/mo | Touch money: be fundable, apply to paid work, join members-tables, propose to the pool |
| Host | $0 | Free to host, always |
| Community | $0 | Free to exist on the platform |

**The platform earns one way: 10% of any money that moves.** Dues, backings, donations passed through, cohort and class fees — 10% each. No rent, no base fees, no per-seat licenses.

**Where a member's $10 goes:** $1 platform · $4 their home community (their pick, changeable) · $5 the project pool.

**Why the free-table rule exists:** communities are free to join, so every community keeps at least one room a free person can actually enter. Otherwise "free to join" is a lie. (Open question: hard rule, or a default a community can turn off? It's one of the few places we override community autonomy.)

**Why a member upgrades:** *you can always show up free — you pay when you want your work funded.* This pitch only works if money is actually flowing, so the growth order is patrons and partners first, members second.

**The trade-off we accepted, stated plainly for the room:** 100 members earns the platform $100/mo. Real platform revenue arrives only when funding moves — one $500 fellowship is $50, a 12-person $120 cohort is $144. We chose an incentive (we earn when creatives get paid) over a rent business, and we should say so out loud.

**What changed from earlier drafts:** an earlier model charged non-charging hosts $50/mo and routed unclaimed community shares to the platform. Both are gone — dropped because they taxed exactly the free communities we most want, and because there is no default community anymore.

### 5.8 The name
**creatives.exchange** is neutral, describes the function, and works as infrastructure under other people's brands.

Two cautions, for the record rather than as objections:
- This is the **fourth name** (TheCrossBoard → The Exchange → The Garden → creatives.exchange). "Exchange" was the original working name; "The Garden" was Haley's and won on warmth. Worth knowing we're partly returning to ground already walked.
- "Exchange" reads transactional. That's *correct* for infrastructure and *wrong* for the feeling we've been building toward. If communities carry the warmth and the platform carries the function, that split works — but it should be a deliberate choice, not a drift.

**Also to settle: does a member think they joined creatives.exchange, or their community?** Skool is invisible plumbing; Substack is semi-visible. That answer determines whether we're a consumer brand or infrastructure — and it changes what we build next.

### 5.9 How someone actually gets into a table

Worth walking through live, because this is where the model either feels real or doesn't:

| Path | How it works | Built? |
|---|---|---|
| **They browse** | Open tables and public events are visible with no account, across every community | Yes — `/tables` |
| **Someone invites them** | The host sends a link or QR that opens straight onto the table | Yes — invite kit |
| **They come to an event first** | The public event is the front door; the ongoing table is what they stay for | Yes — `/garden/events`, guest RSVP |
| **They follow a person** | They see someone's work, then see what that person is part of | **Partly** — portfolios, search, and messaging exist in the core app, but the link from a profile to that person's tables isn't built |
| **A sponsor seats them** | A church buys seats and hands out a code; redeeming lands them seated | Yes — `/c/CODE` |

**What it costs to walk in:** open table — free, no account · members table — needs a seat ($10/mo) · cohort — its own price, set by the host · public event — free, no account.

**The unresolved piece:** if a Rabbit Room member wants into an Abiding Practice members-table, does their seat carry across, or do they need AP membership too? That is §5.3, and this is where it bites.

## 6 · What's already real

Not slideware — this is running in production today:

- One account, profiles, **portfolios, people search, and direct messaging** (long since built in the core app — only just surfaced in the new navigation)
- Projects, and events with public no-account RSVP
- **Communities as first-class objects** with their own tables, sessions, and pages
- **Capability-based entitlements** enforced server-side, with 237 automated tests over the rules
- **Coverage codes** — a church buys N seats, members claim them by link or QR
- **A public funding ledger** — every allocation visible without an account
- Stripe subscription plumbing (checkout, webhooks, reconciliation) awaiting keys

What's *not* built: cross-community discovery, per-community theming and vocabulary, multi-community membership economics — all of which depend on the answers above.

## 7 · What we'd want from a founding community

1. A real gathering that already exists, with people in it now.
2. One person who will host — not administer, host.
3. Willingness to publish funding in the open.
4. Honesty about what we'd be replacing versus complementing.
