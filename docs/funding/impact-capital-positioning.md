# Impact capital for a for-profit infrastructure company that partners with nonprofits

*How well theCreative.exchange can pursue Prebys-style impact investment and adjacent money; what has to be true first. 2026-09-29, owner: Rick. Companion to [prebys-creative-industry-survey-response.md](prebys-creative-industry-survey-response.md) and the [civic partners social graph](../civic-partners-social-graph.md).*

## The short answer

We are a good fit for **impact investment** and a poor fit for **grants**, and we should stop blurring the two. Prebys's survey is explicitly about investing in for-profit creative businesses that deliver measurable impact; that is us. Foundation grants, City and County money, and most private foundations go to 501(c)(3)s; those go to Abiding Practice and The Table Art Society, with the platform as a line in their budgets. One more rule of that world: foundations do not fund regranting pools, they fund managed programs. So the nonprofit side is a fellowship, commission and cohort program with selection, stipends and reported outcomes, and its administration is what a foundation pays for. The structure that makes this work is the one we already have: **the company sells infrastructure and earns fees; a nonprofit partner owns the charitable money and the mission-specific grantmaking; investors buy the company.** GoFundMe and GoFundMe.org, Classy, Givebutter and Open Collective all run on this split, and every serious funder has seen it.

What we are missing is not a structure but the paperwork and proof that make the structure legible: a signed administration agreement with AP, a capital number tied to the financial model, an impact-measurement plan, a PBC path, and a deck. This memo is the list.

## How an impact investor will read us

**What works in our favor**
- A marketplace take rate (10% of money moved) is a return model investors understand; nothing about it is charitable.
- The impact is not a story bolted on; it is the transaction itself. Every dollar we earn is a dollar that reached a creative, a host or a grant.
- We measure what they want counted: dollars to creatives, creatives paid, grants made, covered seats, events. Upstart's five categories map onto this without contortion.
- The nonprofit partnership lowers our risk rather than raising it: AP holds the charity, issues receipts and decides grants; we never touch a donation's deductibility.
- Civic timing: San Diego just showed it will move money to keep creative work alive (the $10.35M restoration, Artists Count, the Art + Design District). We are the rail that moves money to named creatives month after month.

**What works against us, and the answer**

| Concern | Answer |
|---|---|
| Pre-revenue; a founder building at night | The product is live with 237 server tests and 456 commits this year; two founding communities; a public launch on Nov 6; a civic map of 190 nodes. The raise buys the first community hire and a partial founder salary. |
| Two-sided marketplace cold start | Partners first, members second: churches buy seats in bulk, patrons back named work, hosts keep 90%. The Medium scenario is 400 paying members in year one, not 4,000. |
| "Is this a ministry?" | The platform is open to any creative. The Garden is one community and where it started. The purpose statement is "human creativity in service of human flourishing." The house rules are civic: no harassment, no fraud, credit your sources, disclose AI. |
| "Is the company skimming donations?" | Two pots. Business money: dues, backings, class fees. Charity money: gifts to a fund, received by AP, ~87–90% granted, AP takes 5% for administration and the platform 5% as a fee. The two never mix, and backing a creative is never called a donation. |
| "Where is the moat?" | Curation and relationships, not features: provisioned communities, a review team for grants, in-person tables, and a disclosure rule ("made by humans") no incumbent will adopt. |
| Payments and compliance | Stripe processes; Stripe Connect pays out; no customer funds held; 1099s at $600; no chance-based mechanics; $50 payout minimum. |
| Entity | LLC now; Delaware PBC at the first priced investment (the PBC lets us weigh mission against profit and is the form impact investors expect). |
| Founder equity to partners | Haley and David hold stakes in the company, never the nonprofit; documented in People and Pay. |

## What has to be true before we pitch (October)

1. **The AP administration agreement, signed.** AP administers The Garden's grant fund, issues receipts, decides grants, takes 5%; the platform takes 5%; grants are listed on the fund page. Confirm AP's legal entity and EIN first (the IRS record found under that name lists Paradise, CA).
2. **One capital number.** The financial model's Medium scenario needs ~$173k through year two and ~$320k through year three; Small needs ~$88k. Choose the raise (working figure: $250–350k over 18 months) and the instrument (post-money SAFE; PBC conversion on a priced round). Put the same number in the survey, the deck and the terms summary.
3. **The impact-measurement plan, one page.** Five metrics, definitions, cadence (quarterly), and where each comes from in the app. Offer to align with Artists Count.
4. **The deck** (the Slides artifact) and a **data room**: the plan, the business model, the financial model, claims.md, the partner and civic maps, the AP agreement, the cap table, and the Nov 6 program.
5. **Language discipline.** Everything we say about money comes from `docs/marketing/claims.md`. No "public ledger," no "you keep 100%," no promises to a person.
6. **The PBC decision** on paper, even if the conversion waits for a term sheet.
7. **A 90-day proof plan:** Nov 6 backings and covered seats; first grants listed on a fund page; three hosts paid; one church seat purchase.
